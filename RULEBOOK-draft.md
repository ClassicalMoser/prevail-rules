### How to use this book

**Never played before?** Part I is all you need. It sets up your first battle and walks you through it. Twenty minutes, and it is easier with the pieces in front of you.

**Playing already?** Part II is the full ruleset, numbered so you can find and cite any rule. Part III has a one-page summary, a list of the situations that cause arguments, and the unit roster.

Throughout, a reference like §4.3.2 points to a numbered rule in Part II. You can ignore them on a first read.

# PART I — LEARN TO PLAY

## 1. The short version

Two armies stand on a grid. You each command some troops and one general.

Every round, you both play a card face down and turn it over together. The card says what orders you are allowed to give. You give them. Units walk forward, bump into each other, and fight.

Fighting costs you cards.

**When a player's hand of cards runs out, that player loses.**

That is the whole game. Everything below is how it works in practice.

## 2. Set up your first battle

**The board.** Put it between you. It is wider than it is deep; you each sit at a long edge. It needs no markings — nothing in this game is described by naming a square.

**Your armies.** For a first game, use these two. They are legal Mini armies and they match each other closely.

| White — Rome     |     | Black — Carthage       |     |
| ---------------- | --- | ---------------------- | --- |
| Manipular Legion | ×3  | Punic Citizen Spearmen | ×3  |
| Equites          | ×1  | Punic Citizen Cavalry  | ×1  |
| **92 points**    |     | **88 points**          |     |

**Your cards.** Take eight command cards: two showing initiative 1, two showing 2, two showing 3, two showing 4. For a first game, prefer cards whose command says _Movement_. These eight cards are your hand for the whole battle. There is no pile to draw from — what you hold is what you get, and the only way to get spent cards back is to rally, which you will meet in Phase 5.

**Deploy.** Place your units in the three rows nearest your own edge, one to a space. Stay clear of the two columns at each end — those are the open flanks, and nobody starts in them. Point every unit at the enemy.

**Your general.** Put it anywhere in those same three rows. It can share a space with one of your units.

**Black has initiative** for the first round.

## 3. Your units

A unit is a whole formation of men — a legion, a squadron of horse, a screen of skirmishers. Not one soldier.

Four numbers tell you what it can do:

|                 |                                         |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| **Attack**      | how hard it hits                        |
| **Speed**       | how many spaces it can step in one move |
| **Flexibility** | how many times it can turn in one move  |
| **Range**       | how far it can shoot, if at all         |

Three more tell you what it takes to break it. These are **thresholds**: hit a unit hard enough to reach one, and that happens. Nothing carries over between blows — a unit is either fine or it is gone.

|             |                                          |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| **Retreat** | it gives ground                          |
| **Reverse** | it is spun round to face the wrong way   |
| **Rout**    | it breaks and leaves the battle for good |

They climb in that order, and they are printed in that order.

Finally, **Morale** is what a unit's rout costs you: discard that many cards from your hand. Cheap skirmishers cost nothing. Good troops hurt.

### Facing is the whole game

Every unit points one of eight ways, and you must always be able to tell which. This matters more than any number on the unit.

```
@@FIG facing-p1@@
        ↑  the way it is facing

     ·   ·   ·      ← its three front spaces
     ·  [U]  ·      ← its two flanking spaces
     ·   ·   ·      ← its three back spaces
```

Three things follow from it, and you will use all three in your first game:

- A unit **only ever walks forwards**. To go anywhere else, it turns first.
- A friendly unit only helps you fight if it is **looking at the fight**.
- Being caught **from behind** does not give the enemy an advantage. It kills you outright.

## 4. Your cards

A card has no rules text on it. It is a set of printed values, and it is doing several jobs at once. For your first game you only need two of them.

**Initiative, 1 to 4.** Low goes first.

**The command.** What you may order this round — for example _4 units, Movement_, or _2 lines, Ranged Attack_.

The other three matter, and you can pick them up as you play:

- a **round effect**, a bonus that applies all round to your units near your general;
- **unit support**, which decides how large an army your hand can hold together;
- and a **stat it can be committed for**, letting you throw the card away for +1 at a critical moment.

## 5. The round

Five phases, in this order, every round.

|       | Phase         | Who goes first            |
| ----- | ------------- | ------------------------- |
| **1** | Play cards    | both at once              |
| **2** | Move generals | initiative player         |
| **3** | Give orders   | initiative player         |
| **4** | Fight         | initiative player chooses |
| **5** | Clean up      | initiative player         |

### Phase 1 — Play cards

Both of you pick a card from hand and put it face down. Turn them over together.

**The lower initiative number wins initiative** for this round and goes first in every phase that follows. On a tie, whoever already had it keeps it.

Your card stays face up in front of you all round. You are stuck with it now.

### Phase 2 — Move generals

Initiative player first, then the other.

Your general moves up to **4 spaces**, any direction, and can walk over and stop on your own units. It cannot enter a space with an enemy in it. It can also stay where it is.

Generals never fight. They are there because many cards only reach units standing near them.

### Phase 3 — Give orders

Initiative player gives **all** their orders and carries them out. Then the other player does the same.

**How many orders you get.** Read your card. _4 units_ means you may order up to four of your units — fewer is fine, none is fine. _2 lines_ means two orders, each aimed at a whole **line**: your units standing shoulder to shoulder in a row, all facing the same way (or exactly opposite), up to eight of them at once. A line is how you move an army instead of a unit.

A unit cannot be ordered twice in a round, and a unit already locked in a fight cannot be ordered at all.

**Moving a unit.** Spend its two allowances in any order you like:

- **Speed** — one point per space stepped. A unit steps only in the direction it faces.
- **Flexibility** — one point per turn, however far it turns. It may turn once before its first step, and once after each step.

You cannot walk through an enemy, and you cannot stop on a friend. You _can_ walk through a friend if your Flexibility and theirs add up to 4 or more.

> _A Manipular Legion has Speed 2, Flexibility 2. It can step two spaces straight ahead. Or turn, step, turn, step — spending both of each. It cannot step twice and turn twice._

**Walking into the enemy.** Step into a space with an enemy in it and you are **engaged**: you both stand in that one space until the fight resolves in Phase 4. What you get depends on which way the two of you end up facing.

| You are facing…               | What happens                                                                                   |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **opposite** the enemy        | Head on. If it is faster than you it may back off; otherwise you fight in Phase 4.             |
| **across** the enemy          | You hit its flank. It is dragged round to face you, cannot back off, and you fight in Phase 4. |
| **the same way** as the enemy | You came from behind. It routs on the spot. No fight at all.                                   |

That last row is why facing is the whole game.

> **Shooting.** If your card's command says _Ranged Attack_ instead of _Movement_, your ordered units with Range shoot rather than move. Each picks one enemy in the widening cone of spaces ahead of it. Several units ordered by the same card can gang up on one target: one shoots, the rest add +1 each instead of shooting themselves. Skip this on your first game if your cards let you.

### Phase 4 — Fight

Every engaged space fights. The initiative player picks the order.

Both units in a fight attack **at the same time**. Both can break. There is no attacker and defender.

For each of them, add up:

|                |                                              |
| -------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| its **Attack** | the number on the unit                       |
| **support**    | see below                                    |
| **+1**         | if that player throws away a card to help it |
| any bonus      | from its own card this round                 |

**Support** is where the fight is usually won. Look at each friendly unit standing next to the fighter, as long as it is not behind it:

- it is **looking at the fight** → **+2**
- the fight is **off to its left or right** → **+1**
- it is looking away, or it is stuck in its own fight → **nothing**

Now take each unit's total and compare it against **the other unit's** three thresholds, left to right:

- reaches its **Retreat** → it gives ground
- reaches its **Reverse** → it is spun round
- reaches its **Rout** → it is removed, and its owner discards Morale cards

If it routs, stop — the other two no longer matter.

> **A worked fight.** _Your Legion (Attack 4) charges a Spearmen unit (Attack 3). Two more of your Legions stand beside the fight: one is looking straight at it, one is side-on. That is +2 and +1, so your total is 4 + 3 = **7**. The Spearmen have nobody beside them: their total is **3**._
>
> _Spearmen thresholds are Retreat 6, Reverse 7, Rout 8. Your 7 reaches Retreat and Reverse but not Rout — so they give ground, then spin round. Badly placed now._
>
> _Your Legion's thresholds are Retreat 5, Reverse 6, Rout 8. Their 3 reaches none of them. Your Legion is untouched._
>
> _The two supporting Legions did nothing but stand in the right place facing the right way, and they doubled the blow._

### Phase 5 — Clean up

Put your face-up card onto your **played pile**. It is not in your hand any more.

Now each player, initiative first, decides whether to **rally**. Rallying is the only way to get cards back.

**If you rally:** take one card from your played pile at random and **burn** it — it is gone from the game for good. Take everything else back into your hand: the rest of the played pile, and everything you discarded.

Then check that your cards can still hold your army together. Every card can steady a certain number of units, and says which sort. Add all that up and give each unit on the board one place. Any unit you cannot find a place for **routs**, and you pay its Morale.

**If you do not rally:** nothing happens. Your cards stay where they are and nobody checks your units. You just have fewer cards next round.

That is the central squeeze of the game. You need your cards back, and getting them back costs one card forever and puts your army to the test.

## 6. Go and play

Start the next round. Play three or four before you worry about playing well.

When something comes up that this part does not answer — exactly where a retreating unit may go, whether a diagonal is blocked, how far a line can stretch — it is in Part II under the obvious heading. Part III, section 13, is a list of the questions that actually come up at the table, in the words people use to ask them.

# PART II — THE RULES

## 1. Golden rules

**1.1** Cards carry no rules text. Everything a card does is printed as values — an initiative number, a command, a round effect, a support grant, a commit stat — and this book governs how those values are read. A card never overrides a rule.

**1.2** If a rule says you _may_ do a thing, you may always decline. If it says you _must_, you must, and if you cannot, see **§11**.

**1.3** Where two effects would resolve at the same moment and the order matters, the **initiative player resolves first** (§4.1.5).

**1.4** All information on the board is open. Hands are secret. A card played face down in Phase 1 is secret until revealed. Committed cards are revealed as they are committed.

**1.5** Nothing is remembered between rounds except what is physically on the table: positions, facings, card piles, and whose initiative it is. There is no tracking of damage, fatigue, or previous orders.

## 2. The battlefield

### 2.1 The board

**2.1.1** The board is a rectangular grid of square spaces, wider than it is deep. The two players face each other across it from the long edges. Depth is measured between the players; frontage runs across.

**2.1.2 No notation is needed.** Positions in this book are always described relative to a unit — the space ahead of it, the space behind it, its flanking spaces — and never by a fixed name. An unmarked mat is enough to play on. A digital board or a printed one may label its rows and columns as a convenience, but no rule depends on those labels and you never have to read one out.

**2.1.3** Three board sizes exist. Each is given as **depth × frontage**:

| Board    | Size    | Spaces | Used by        |
| -------- | ------- | ------ | -------------- |
| Small    | 8 × 12  | 96     | Tutorial, Mini |
| Standard | 12 × 18 | 216    | Standard       |
| Large    | 24 × 36 | 864    | Epic           |

### 2.2 Occupancy

**2.2.1** A space holds **at most one unit**, with one exception: two enemy units may occupy the same space while **engaged** (§6). Such a space is an **engaged space**.

**2.2.2** Commanders do not occupy space. A commander may share a space with a friendly unit, and more than one thing may stand in a space if one of them is a commander.

**2.2.3** No unit may enter or pass through an engaged space, for any reason, until that engagement is resolved.

### 2.3 Facing

**2.3.1** Every unit faces one of eight directions: N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW. Mark facing clearly — the whole game turns on it.

**2.3.2** Relative to a unit's facing, the eight surrounding spaces divide into three groups:

- **Front spaces** — three: the space it faces and the two to either side of that (45° off).
- **Flanking spaces** — two: directly to its left and right (90° off).
- **Back spaces** — three: the remaining spaces behind it.

```
@@FIG facing@@
        ↑  the way it is facing

     ·   ·   ·      ← its three front spaces
     ·  [U]  ·      ← its two flanking spaces
     ·   ·   ·      ← its three back spaces
```

**2.3.3 Spaces behind.** Beyond the three back spaces, the phrase _the spaces behind a unit_ means the whole region of the board extending backward from those three spaces — the wedge at the unit's back. This matters for retreats (§9.1) and rear attacks (§6.3.3).

### 2.4 Adjacency, distance, and the forward arc

**2.4.1 Adjacent** means orthogonally or diagonally touching — up to eight spaces.

**2.4.2 Distance** is counted in steps where a diagonal step counts as **one**. "Within 3 spaces" means within three such steps.

**2.4.3 The forward arc** is used for shooting (§7.4). Begin with the shooter's three front spaces; that is arc 1. For arc 2, add the three front spaces of each of those, and so on out to the shooter's Range. The result is a widening cone. The shooter's own space is not in its arc.

**2.4.4 Blocked diagonals.** Two enemy units standing in both of the orthogonal spaces that flank a diagonal **block** that diagonal. One enemy unit does not. A blocked diagonal cannot be stepped across (§5.4) and cannot carry support (§7.3.2).

```
@@FIG diagonal@@
   A diagonal step passes between the two
   spaces that flank the corner you cut.

   Enemies in BOTH of them:  blocked.
   An enemy in only one:     fine.
```

## 3. Armies and setup

### 3.1 Building an army

**3.1.1** An army is a list of units and a set of command cards, built to the limits of the chosen game mode.

**3.1.2** The cards you choose **are** your hand. You hold all of them from the first round. There is no pile to draw from, nothing in reserve, and no way to add a card once the battle has begun.

**3.1.3** Mode limits:

|                                  | Tutorial / Mini | Standard | Epic  |
| -------------------------------- | --------------- | -------- | ----- |
| Board                            | Small           | Standard | Large |
| Max total unit cost              | 100             | 200      | 300   |
| Max different unit types         | 4               | 8        | 12    |
| **Minimum total Morale**         | 8               | 12       | 16    |
| Cards of _each_ initiative value | 2               | 3        | 4     |
| Total cards in hand              | 8               | 12       | 16    |

**3.1.4** Each unit type shows a **limit** — the most copies of it one army may field (never more than 8).

**3.1.5** No unit type may be listed twice, and no command card may appear twice in one army.

**3.1.6 The morale floor is a leash.** You must field at least the minimum total Morale, which means you cannot build an army of cheap, worthless troops that costs you nothing when it dies. Somebody in your list has to matter.

**3.1.7 The initiative spread is also a leash.** You must take an equal number of cards at each initiative value 1 through 4. No army can be built to always go first.

### 3.2 Deployment

**3.2.1** Decide which long edge each player takes. One is **White**, one is **Black**.

**3.2.2** Your **deployment zone** is the band of **three rows nearest your own edge**, less the **outermost two columns at each end**. The same rule gives the zone on every board size; only the width changes.

| Board    | Zone        | Frontage       |
| -------- | ----------- | -------------- |
| Small    | 3 rows deep | 8 spaces wide  |
| Standard | 3 rows deep | 14 spaces wide |
| Large    | 3 rows deep | 32 spaces wide |

The two columns at each end lie outside both deployment zones. They are the open flanks: nobody begins there, and getting into them is most of what fast troops are for.

**3.2.3** Place every unit in your army in your zone, one per space, each facing any direction you choose.

**3.2.4** Place your commander on any space in your zone. It may share that space with one of your own units.

**3.2.5** **Black holds initiative** going into round one. Play begins at Phase 1, where the cards may take it away again.

> **Designer's note.** Deployment is deliberately crude in this draft: a flat belt, both sides symmetrical, no scenario terrain, no screening rules, no hidden setup. Scenario-specific zones are the obvious next thing to build.

## 4. The round

A battle is fought in rounds. Each round has five phases, resolved in order, and no phase may be skipped — though a phase with nothing in it ends immediately.

### 4.1 Phase 1 — Play Cards

**4.1.1** Both players **simultaneously and secretly** choose one card from hand and place it face down.

**4.1.2** Reveal both cards together. Each is now **in play** and stays face up in front of its owner for the whole round.

**4.1.3** Compare initiative values. **The lower value takes initiative.**

**4.1.4** On a **tie**, initiative stays with whoever already had it.

**4.1.5** The player with initiative is the **initiative player** for the rest of the round and acts first in every phase that has an order.

**4.1.6** From this moment until Cleanup your revealed card is doing two jobs: it grants its Command (§4.3) and it imposes its Round Effect (§4.3.7). It is no longer in your hand, so it cannot be committed (§8.6). Its unit support is unaffected — support is only ever measured after a rally, when every card you still own is back in your hand (§10.4).

**⚠ Edge case.** A card in play cannot be committed (§8). Once revealed, it is spoken for.

### 4.2 Phase 2 — Move Commanders

**4.2.1** The initiative player moves their commander, then the other player moves theirs.

**4.2.2** A commander may move up to **4 spaces**. Diagonal steps count as one.

**4.2.3** A commander has no facing and may change direction freely mid-move.

**4.2.4** A commander may enter and pass through any space **not containing an enemy unit** — including empty spaces and spaces holding friendly units. It may not enter a space holding an enemy unit, and therefore may not enter an engaged space.

**4.2.5** A commander may not cross a diagonal blocked by enemies (§2.4.4).

**4.2.6** A commander may stay where it is.

**4.2.7 Why this matters.** A card's round effect usually carries an **inspiration range** measured from your commander, and a few commands do too (§4.3.4). Where a range is printed, your general's position decides which of your troops it reaches.

> **Open question.** This draft gives no way to attack, capture, or lose a commander. The rules engine tolerates a commander being absent from the board and simply treats every inspiration range as unsatisfiable — so the machinery for a commander's death exists, but nothing in the rules can kill one. See §16.6.

### 4.3 Phase 3 — Issue Commands

**The idea, first.** Your card says what you are allowed to order this round and how much of it. It might be _four units_, or it might be _two lines_ — a line being a whole rank of troops moving as one. You spend that allowance on the units you want to act, then you carry the orders out one by one.

The allowance is the interesting part. A card that orders one line can move eight units if they happen to be dressed in a rank; the same card orders nothing at all if your army is scattered. Formation is not decoration here — it is how much you get to do.

This phase has four steps: the initiative player **issues** all their commands, then **resolves** all of them; then the other player does the same.

#### Issuing

**4.3.1** Your in-play card shows exactly one Command, with a **size** and a **number**.

**4.3.2 Size: units.** You make **one** order, selecting **up to** the printed number of eligible units. The number is a ceiling, not a quota — commanding fewer is always allowed, including none.

**4.3.3 Size: lines.** You make that many **separate** orders, each one aimed at a single **line** (§4.3.5). Two lines means two orders, resolved independently.

**4.3.4 Eligibility.** A unit may be ordered only if **it is not engaged** — a unit locked in an engagement cannot be given an order of its own and cannot support a ranged attack until that engagement resolves — and only if it satisfies _all_ of the Command's restrictions:

- **Trait restrictions** — the unit must have **every** listed trait.
- **Unit restrictions** — the unit must be one of the listed types.
- **Inspiration range** — where one is printed, the unit must be within that many spaces of **your own commander** (§2.4.2). Most commands print no range at all and are unrestricted by distance; the field exists for the ones that do.

**⚠ Open at the table.** Whether an **engaged** unit can be counted as part of a _line_ is undecided (§16.13). A line passing through an engaged unit is the case in question; it is not the same as ordering that unit by itself, which §4.3.4 forbids. Agree on a reading before you start and stay with it.

**4.3.5 Lines.** A **line** is a rank of your own units standing shoulder to shoulder:

- Every unit lies in the **flanking space** of the one beside it — that is, the line runs _across_ their facing, not along it.
- Every unit faces the **same direction, or exactly the opposite** direction. A rank that has about-faced is still a rank.
- The line is **unbroken**: no gaps, no enemies, no non-qualifying units in the middle.
- A line is **1 to 8 units** long.

You select a line by naming its two ends. The **near end you name must satisfy the full restrictions** including inspiration range; the **rest of the line need only satisfy the trait and type restrictions**. A line therefore reaches beyond your general's voice, so long as it starts within it.

**4.3.6 One order per unit.** A unit may be commanded only **once per round**, whether as a single unit or as part of a line. Already-commanded units cannot be included in a later line, and a line cannot be drawn _through_ one — it stops there.

**4.3.7 Round Effects are not orders.** A card's Round Effect applies to every one of your units that meets its restrictions, all round, commanded or not. Its restrictions are read separately from the Command's and are usually different — in particular, most round effects do carry an inspiration range even when the command on the same card does not.

**4.3.8 Command modifiers apply only to the commanded.** A Command's printed modifiers (e.g. _+1 Attack, −2 Speed_) apply for the whole round, but **only to units that actually received that order**. They stay in force through Phase 4 — a unit ordered to charge still has its bonus when the melee resolves.

#### Resolving

**4.3.9** Resolve your issued commands one at a time, in any order you like, finishing each before beginning the next.

**4.3.10 Resolving a movement order** (§5):

1. You may **commit** a card for **+1 Speed** or **+1 Flexibility** (§8). Optional.
2. Move the unit.
3. If it moved into an enemy-occupied space, an **engagement** begins and is set up now (§6).

**4.3.11 Resolving a ranged attack order** (§7.4):

1. Choose the firing unit. It must be unengaged and have Range of at least 1.
2. Choose one enemy target within its forward arc (§2.4.3).
3. Optionally name **supporters** — other units you ordered with this same command that have not yet fired, and that could each independently shoot that same target. Each supporter adds **+1**, and naming it spends that unit's own shot (§7.3.4).
4. The **attacker** may commit a card (+1 Range, Attack, or Flexibility). Optional.
5. The **defender** may commit a card (+1 Range, Attack, or Flexibility). Optional.
6. Resolve the attack (§7).

**⚠ Edge case.** An engaged unit can neither shoot nor support a shot. It is busy.

**⚠ Edge case.** Shooting is never automatic. Units with Range do nothing at all unless a Ranged Attack command orders them to fire.

**⚠ Edge case.** **Line of sight is not yet part of the game.** Nothing blocks a shot: intervening units, friendly or enemy, are ignored, and if the target is in the arc and within Range it can be hit. Line of sight is planned, as terrain is (§16.7); until it arrives, play it as written here.

### 4.4 Phase 4 — Resolve Melee

**4.4.1** Every engaged space on the board fights this phase. There is no choice about _whether_.

**4.4.2** The **initiative player** chooses which engagement to resolve, resolves it completely, then chooses the next, until none remain. The choice of order is entirely theirs.

**4.4.3** Resolve each engagement per §7.2.

**4.4.4** If there are no engagements, the phase ends immediately.

### 4.5 Phase 5 — Cleanup

**4.5.1 Discard played cards.** Both players move their in-play card to their **played pile**. It is not in your hand and it is not in your discard; it sits in the played pile until you rally or the game ends.

**4.5.2 The initiative player decides whether to rally**, and fully resolves that decision (§10.3) including the support check and any routs it causes.

**4.5.3 Then the other player** does the same.

**4.5.4** The round ends. A new round begins at Phase 1.

## 5. Movement

**The idea, first.** A unit walks forwards. That is the only thing it can do. Everything else about movement follows from that one restriction: to go anywhere other than straight ahead, a unit must first turn to face the way it wants to go, and turning is a separate, limited resource from walking.

A move is therefore a short sequence of steps and turns, in whatever order you like, paid for out of two allowances that refresh each time the unit is ordered.

### 5.1 The two resources

**5.1.1** A move spends two things: **Speed**, for stepping, and **Flexibility**, for turning. Both refresh every time the unit is ordered — they are not a pool that depletes over the battle.

**5.1.2** Each step into an adjacent space costs **1 Speed**. Diagonal steps cost 1, same as orthogonal.

**5.1.3** A unit only ever steps **in the direction it is currently facing**. To go somewhere else, turn first.

### 5.2 Turning

**5.2.1** A facing change costs **1 Flexibility**, regardless of how far the unit turns. Spinning 180° costs the same as pivoting 45°.

**5.2.2** A unit may change facing **once before its first step, and once after each step it takes**. It cannot turn twice on the spot.

**5.2.3** A unit with Flexibility 0 cannot turn at all. It moves in a straight line or not at all.

### 5.3 What you may move into

A unit may **end its move** in a space that is:

- **empty**, or
- **occupied by a single enemy unit**, if it may legally engage it (§6.2).

A unit may **never** end its move in a space occupied by a friendly unit, nor in an engaged space.

### 5.4 What you may move through

**5.4.1** Empty spaces: freely.

**5.4.2** Spaces with a **friendly** unit: only if **your Flexibility plus theirs is 4 or more**, using current values including all modifiers. Two stiff formations cannot interpenetrate; nimble troops can filter through.

**5.4.3** Spaces with an **enemy** unit: never.

**5.4.4** **Engaged spaces**: never.

**5.4.5 Diagonal steps** additionally require that at least **one** of the two orthogonal spaces flanking the diagonal be one you could pass through. You cannot slip between two units standing corner to corner, and you cannot cross a diagonal blocked by enemies (§2.4.4). The step still costs only 1 Speed.

**⚠ Edge case.** If you pass through several friendly units in one move, check the combined-Flexibility test **separately for each one**.

**⚠ Edge case.** A unit may be ordered and then not move at all — a legal move of zero steps and zero turns. It still counts as commanded, and still receives the Command's modifiers (§4.3.8). Ordering a unit to stand still and gain +1 Attack is a real and often correct play.

## 6. Engagements

**The idea, first.** Units do not stand next to each other and trade blows across a boundary. To fight, one unit walks into the other's space, and the two of them share it until the matter is settled. That shared space is an engagement.

Two questions follow, and the rest of this section answers them. _May I walk in at all?_ — which depends on the side I approach from. _And what kind of fight is it?_ — which depends on which way the two of us end up facing. Coming at a unit from behind does not merely give you an advantage in the fight; it means there is no fight.

### 6.1 What an engagement is

**6.1.1** An **engagement** is created the moment a unit ends a step in a space occupied by a single enemy unit. Both units now stand in that one space. It is an **engaged space**.

**6.1.2** Engagements are created in Phase 3 and fought in Phase 4. Between those moments the two units are locked together: neither can be commanded again, neither can shoot, neither can support anything, and nothing may enter or cross their space.

**6.1.3** The unit that moved in is the **engaging unit**. The unit that was already there is the **defender**.

### 6.2 When you may engage

Look at which of the defender's spaces you are stepping **from**:

**6.2.1 From a flanking space:** always allowed.

**6.2.2 From a back space:** allowed **only if your unit began this entire move in the region behind the defender** (§2.3.3). You cannot loop around a formation's flank and take it in the back in the same order. You must already have been behind it.

**6.2.3 From a front space:** allowed only if your unit **ends its move facing directly opposite the defender** — squared up, head on. If it is not already facing that way it must spend 1 Flexibility to turn as it closes.

### 6.3 What kind of engagement it is

**6.3.1** Compare the **engaging unit's final facing** with the **defender's facing**. Note that this is facing against facing, not position.

| Engaging unit's facing, relative to defender's | Type      |
| ---------------------------------------------- | --------- |
| The same, or 45° off either way                | **Rear**  |
| 90° off either way                             | **Flank** |
| Directly opposite                              | **Front** |

Read it as a picture: if the two units are looking at each other, that is a Front engagement. If one is looking across the other, that is a Flank. If they are both looking the same way, the attacker has come from behind.

**6.3.2 Front engagement.** The defender may be offered the chance to fall back:

1. The defender may **commit** a card for +1 Speed or +1 Flexibility (§8). Optional.
2. Compare current **Speed**. The defender may retreat **only if its Speed is strictly greater** than the engaging unit's.
3. If it may and chooses to, it makes a retreat move (§9.1). The engagement ends at once; no melee is fought.
4. Otherwise both units stay locked and fight in Phase 4.

**6.3.3 Rear engagement.** The defender is **routed immediately** (§9.3). No melee, no commitment, no reply. Its owner pays its Morale at once. The engaging unit remains where it stands.

**6.3.4 Flank engagement.** The defender is **forced to rotate** to face the engaging unit — its new facing is directly opposite the engaging unit's. It may not retreat. The two fight in Phase 4.

A flank attack does no damage by itself. What it does is remove the defender's options: it cannot fall back, and it enters the melee having just been turned, which usually costs it the support of the units beside it (§7.3.1).

**⚠ Edge case.** A defender that is routed by a rear engagement pays Morale immediately, in Phase 3 — not in Phase 4, and not at Cleanup.

**⚠ Edge case.** If several of your units engage in one phase, each engagement is fully set up as its movement resolves. A unit that has already been engaged by one of your units cannot be engaged again — its space is now an engaged space and nothing may enter it (§2.2.3). You get one attacker per defender.

## 7. Combat

All combat, melee or ranged, works the same way: build an attack value, compare it to the target's three thresholds, apply what it reaches.

### 7.1 Attack value

**7.1.1** Start with the unit's printed **Attack**.

**7.1.2** Apply, in this order, every modifier that applies:

1. **Round Effect** of the attacker's own in-play card, if the unit meets its restrictions (§4.3.7).
2. **Command modifiers**, if the unit was commanded this round (§4.3.8).
3. **Committed card**, if one was committed for this combat (§8).

**7.1.3** Then add **support** (§7.3).

**7.1.4** The result is the attack value. Modifiers may be negative; an attack value cannot be reduced below zero.

### 7.2 Melee

**7.2.1** Melee is **mutual and simultaneous**. Both units attack. Both may break. There is no attacker and defender in a melee, only two units in the same space.

**7.2.2** Procedure for one engagement:

1. The **initiative player** may commit a card for their unit (+1 Attack, Defence, or Flexibility). Optional.
2. The **other player** may then do the same. Optional.
3. Compute both attack values (§7.1), each including that side's own melee support.
4. Compare each unit's attack value against the **other** unit's thresholds.
5. Apply results to both units (§7.5).

**7.2.3** If neither unit routs or retreats, they remain engaged and will fight again next round.

> _Attacker: Attack 4, +1 from its card's round effect, +3 support = **8**._
> _Defender: Attack 4, +2 support = **6**._
> _Attacker's thresholds — Retreat 5, Reverse 6, Rout 8. Defender's — Retreat 5, Reverse 6, Rout 7._
> _8 meets the defender's Rout: the defender breaks._
> _6 meets the attacker's Retreat and Reverse but not its Rout: the attacker falls back, then turns about._

### 7.3 Support

**The idea, first.** A unit does not fight alone. The friends standing around it add to its blow — and how much they add depends on whether they are actually facing the fight or looking somewhere else.

This is usually the largest number in the calculation, often larger than the unit's own Attack. It is also the number you have the most control over, because it is decided entirely by where your units stand and which way you have pointed them. Melee and ranged support work differently and are described separately below.

#### 7.3.1 Melee support

A friendly unit lends support to one of your units in melee if **all** of these are true:

- it is **adjacent** to your fighting unit;
- it is **not** in one of your fighting unit's **back spaces**;
- it is **not itself engaged**;
- if it is diagonally adjacent, that diagonal is **not blocked** (§2.4.4).

How much it lends depends on **which way the supporter is facing**:

| The fighting unit lies in the supporter's… | Support |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------- |
| **front spaces**                           | **+2**  |
| **flanking spaces**                        | **+1**  |
| back spaces                                | none    |

**7.3.2** Add up every qualifying supporter. There is no cap. A unit fighting in the middle of a formed rank commonly gains +4 or more.

```
@@FIG support@@
      [S1]  [S2]
         \  /
         [ U ]  ──▶ engaged here

   S1 faces toward U      → +2
   S2 faces across U      → +1
   U fights at Attack + 3
```

**7.3.3** Support is recalculated fresh for each combat, from the board as it stands at that moment. Units that routed earlier in the phase are simply not there any more.

#### 7.3.4 Ranged support

Ranged support is much simpler and quite different from melee support. Each supporting unit adds **+1**, flat, regardless of facing or position.

A supporter must be one of your units that

- was **ordered by this same Ranged Attack command**,
- has **not yet fired** its own shot this phase, and
- could **independently make that same shot** — unengaged, Range 1 or more, the target within its own forward arc.

Naming a unit as a supporter **spends its shot**. A command that orders four archers may therefore be fired as four separate attacks, or as a single attack at +3, or anything in between. Concentrating fire is how you break a hard target; spreading it is how you cover a frontage.

### 7.4 Ranged attacks

**7.4.1** The firing unit must be **unengaged** and have current **Range** of at least 1.

**7.4.2** The target must be an enemy unit in the firer's **forward arc** (§2.4.3) out to its current Range. Nothing blocks the shot; line of sight is not yet in the game (§4.3.11, §16.7).

**7.4.3** Attack value = the firer's Attack (with its modifiers, §7.1) + 1 per supporter (§7.3.4).

**7.4.4** Ranged fire is **one way**. The target does not shoot back.

**7.4.5** A card committed by the **defender** against a ranged attack raises that unit's **thresholds** for this attack — it is genuinely defensive, unlike a melee commitment.

### 7.5 Applying the result

**7.5.1** Compare the attack value against each of the target's three thresholds. A threshold is met if the attack value is **equal to or greater than** it. The three are printed on the unit in the order they resolve, left to right:

| Threshold met | Result                                                                                    |
| ------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Retreat**   | The unit falls back (§9.1).                                                               |
| **Reverse**   | The unit is turned 180° on the spot (§9.2).                                               |
| **Rout**      | The unit is removed from the battle; its owner discards cards equal to its Morale (§9.3). |

**7.5.2** An attack will often meet more than one threshold. **Work left to right, in the printed order: retreat, then reverse, then rout.**

**7.5.3 A rout saves you the trouble.** If the attack reaches the Rout threshold, the unit is leaving the board, and whether it would also have fallen back or turned about changes nothing. Do not bother working them out. Take the unit off and pay its Morale.

**7.5.4** A unit that retreats and reverses does both, in that order: it falls back, then turns about. Falling back first is often what makes the reverse possible at all — see 7.5.5.

**7.5.5** **Reverse cannot happen while a unit is still engaged.** If a unit in a melee is reversed but does not retreat, and its opponent likewise stays in the space, the reverse simply does not occur — there is no room to turn about with an enemy in your face.

**⚠ Edge case.** A unit that **must** retreat but **has no legal retreat** (§9.1.5) **routs instead** — the one case where a result early in the order escalates into the last one. It is among the most dangerous situations in the game, and the reason a unit's back should never be against something it cannot cross.

## 8. Commitments

**The idea, first.** A commitment is how you lean on a unit in a moment that matters. Throw a card out of your hand and that unit does a little better than it should have: one point of attack, of defence, of speed, of flexibility, of range — whichever the card names.

It is always optional, it is always worth exactly +1, and the card comes back at your next rally. What you give up is not the card but the use of it in the meantime.

**8.1** A **commitment** is discarding one card from your hand to squeeze +1 out of a unit for one action. Committed cards go to your **discard pile** and come back when you rally (§10.3).

**8.2** A card names the stat it may be committed for — normally exactly one. Committing gives **+1** to that stat. The value is never anything but +1. A card offering a choice of two stats is possible but uncommon; where one does, choose as you commit.

**8.3** Commitment is **always optional** and always declinable, including when your opponent has already committed.

**8.4** Each moment accepts only certain stats, so a card is committable there only if its stat is one of them. A card whose stat is Range is no use in a melee.

| Moment                                     | Stats it accepts             | Who commits first                 |
| ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Before a movement order resolves (§4.3.10) | Speed, Flexibility           | The moving player                 |
| Defending a front engagement (§6.3.2)      | Speed, Flexibility           | The defender                      |
| A ranged attack (§4.3.11)                  | Range, Attack, Flexibility   | Attacker, then defender           |
| A melee (§7.2.2)                           | Attack, Defence, Flexibility | Initiative player, then the other |

**8.5 Defence** as a commitment stat raises all three of a unit's thresholds by 1, making it harder to make that unit retreat, reverse, or rout.

**8.6** A card in play (§4.1.6) cannot be committed. Only cards in hand can.

**⚠ Edge case.** Commitments come back on your next rally, so a committed card is lent, not spent. What it costs you is the interval: until you rally, that card cannot be played, cannot be committed again, and cannot break a tie for initiative.

## 9. Retreat, reverse, rout

The three things an attack can do to a unit, in the order they resolve (§7.5.2). A unit that is routing makes the other two moot; work them out only if it survives.

### 9.1 Retreat

**9.1.1** A retreat is a **backward move**, made with the unit's own Speed and Flexibility.

**9.1.2** A retreating unit must stay within **the region behind its starting position** (§2.3.3) — both the spaces it passes through and the space it ends in.

**9.1.3** A retreat must be the **smallest legal backward move available**: the fewest facing changes first, then the fewest steps. You do not get to use a retreat as a free repositioning. Where several moves tie for smallest, the owner chooses among them.

**9.1.4** While retreating, a unit may only turn to a facing **45° from its current one** at the start of the move. It cannot about-face while withdrawing.

**9.1.5** A unit **cannot retreat at all** if:

- there is an **unengaged enemy unit in the region behind it that is facing it** — its line of withdrawal is cut; or
- no legal backward move exists — the board edge, friendly units it cannot pass, or enemies are in the way.

**9.1.6** A unit that is required to retreat and cannot, **routs** (§7.5, edge case).

**9.1.7** A retreating unit may not enter an enemy-occupied space. A retreat never creates an engagement.

### 9.2 Reverse

**9.2.1** The unit turns 180° where it stands. Nothing else changes.

**9.2.2** A reverse costs no Flexibility and is not a move.

**9.2.3** It cannot be applied to a unit still engaged (§7.5.5).

**9.2.4** Reverse is the mildest result on paper and often not in practice. A reversed unit's front becomes its back, so the enemy that just struck it is now standing behind it, and a rear engagement next round routs it outright (§6.3.3).

### 9.3 Rout

**9.3.1** A routed unit is **removed from the battle permanently**. It does not come back.

**9.3.2** Its owner immediately discards cards from hand equal to that unit's **Morale**, chosen freely from their hand. Morale 0 costs nothing.

**9.3.3** If several units rout at the same moment — as happens at the support check (§10.4) — sum their Morale and discard that many at once.

**9.3.4** If you cannot pay, you lose (§11.2).

## 10. Cards: hand, piles, rally, support

**The idea, first.** There is one set of cards and it does everything: it is your hand, your losses, your recovery, and the measure of how much army you can keep on the table. Cards move between a few places, and only one of those moves is permanent.

Cards leave your hand three ways — played, committed, or discarded to pay for a rout. All three come back when you rally. Rallying destroys one card, at random, forever. That single burnt card per rally is the only permanent loss in the whole system, and it is the reason a long battle grinds an army down.

### 10.1 The five places a card can be

| Place                                              | In your hand? | Comes back?                   |
| -------------------------------------------------- | ------------- | ----------------------------- |
| **Hand**                                           | yes           | —                             |
| **In play** — this round's card                    | no            | to the played pile at Cleanup |
| **Played pile** — cards played in earlier rounds   | no            | on rally, minus one burnt     |
| **Discard pile** — commitments and Morale payments | no            | on rally, all of it           |
| **Burnt**                                          | no            | **never**                     |

**10.1.1** Only cards **in your hand** can be played or committed.

**10.1.2** Unit support, by contrast, does not depend on where a card is. The support check happens only in the instant after a rally (§10.4), and a rally has just gathered every card you still own into your hand. So a card provides its support unless it has been **burnt**. Burning is the only thing that takes support away.

### 10.2 How cards leave your hand

- **Played** in Phase 1 → in play → played pile.
- **Committed** for a bonus (§8) → discard pile.
- **Discarded to pay Morale** when a unit routs (§9.3) → discard pile.
- **Burnt** during a rally (§10.3) → gone for good.

### 10.3 Rallying

**10.3.1** In Cleanup, each player in turn may choose to rally. It is the **only** way to get cards back into your hand.

**10.3.2** To rally:

1. **Burn** one of your played cards, chosen at random. It is out of the game for good.
2. Take every other card in your played pile back into your hand.
3. Take your **entire discard pile** back into your hand.
4. Immediately perform the **unit support check** (§10.4).

**10.3.3** You cannot rally with an empty played pile — there would be nothing to burn.

**10.3.4** If you decline to rally, nothing happens: your played pile keeps growing and **no support check occurs**. Units stay on the board regardless of whether your hand could hold them.

**10.3.5** Rallying costs you three things: one card destroyed for good, no say in which card that is, and a support check you may not be ready for. Against that, it is the only way to get your hand back, so the question is when, not whether.

### 10.4 The unit support check

**The idea, first.** Your cards are what holds your army on the field. Each card can keep a certain number of units in good order — some cards will steady anything, most are particular about what they steady. When you rally, you check whether your cards can still account for everything you have on the board. Whatever they cannot account for breaks and leaves.

So it is a counting exercise, done once, in one go: _this is what my cards can hold, these are the units I have, anything left over is gone._

Three things make it less alarming than it sounds. It only happens when you rally, so you choose when to face it. Every card you own is in your hand at that moment, so nothing is missing from the count. And the number never drifts downward on its own — the only thing that shrinks it is burning, one card per rally.

#### The check in detail

**10.4.1 When.** Immediately after a rally, and only for the player who rallied (§10.3.2). At no other time.

**10.4.2 What a card holds.** Each card names one kind of unit it can steady, and how many:

- a **named unit type** — _Manipular Legion ×2_
- a **trait** — _phalanx ×1_
- **any unit at all** — _generic ×1_
- or nothing; some cards hold nothing.

**10.4.3 Add them up into pools.** Cards naming the same thing combine. Two cards each holding _Velites ×2_ make a single pool of _Velites ×4_. Cards naming different things stay separate: a _phalanx_ pool and a _generic_ pool are two pools, not one.

**10.4.4 Match your units to the pools.** Every unit on your board needs a place in one pool. A unit may take **only one** place, and only in a pool it matches — a Libyan Spearman can go in a _Libyan Spearmen_ pool, a _phalanx_ pool, or a _generic_ pool, but into exactly one of them.

**10.4.5 Use what you have.** If a place is still open and any uncovered unit could fill it, you must fill it. You are **not** required to find the arrangement that saves the most units. Where a unit could go into either a narrow pool or a broad one, that is your call, and a bad call is allowed to cost you.

**10.4.6 Anything left over routs.** Every unit with no place removes itself from the board, all at once (§9.3.3).

**10.4.7 Pay for them together.** Add up the Morale of everything that just routed and discard that many cards. If you cannot, you lose (§11.2).

> _Your cards hold: Manipular Legion ×2, formation ×1, generic ×1 — four places in three pools._
> _Your board has five units: 3 Manipular Legions, 1 Libyan Spearmen (formation), 1 Velites._
>
> _Five units, four places. Something is going to break; the only question is what._
>
> _The two Legion places take two Legions. The formation place can take the third Legion or the Spearmen — both qualify. The generic place takes whichever of those two is left. The Velites fit nowhere: they match no remaining pool, and they rout. Pay their Morale, which is nothing._
>
> _Note what you could have done instead. Put the Velites in the generic place and the Spearmen in the formation place, and it is the third Legion that routs — two Morale instead of none. That assignment is perfectly legal; §10.4.5 does not oblige you to find the better one._

**10.4.8 What this costs you over a battle.** Nothing you commit and nothing you pay in Morale reduces your support, because those cards return. Only burning does, and you burn exactly one card per rally. An army's capacity to hold itself together therefore declines slowly, predictably, and entirely as a consequence of how often you have needed to rally.

## 11. Ending the game

**11.1 Empty hand.** If a player's hand is empty, that player **loses immediately**. It does not matter how many cards sit in their played pile or discard. If both hands are empty at the same moment, the game is a **draw**.

**11.2 Unpayable Morale.** If a player is required to discard cards for Morale (§9.3) and the number required is **equal to or greater than** the cards in their hand, they **lose immediately**. Note that paying your _last_ card is already a loss — you need to survive the payment, not merely make it.

**11.3 No units left.** A player with no units on the board **loses immediately**, however many cards they are holding. This will almost never come up in a real battle, and it is not there for the cases where it does: it is there to close the door on feeding your own army to the enemy so that you can go on cycling a small hand indefinitely. An army with nothing left to command has lost, whatever its general still has in hand.

**11.4** There is no other victory condition, and in particular no points, no objectives, and no territory. Wiping out an enemy army does end the game (§11.3), but as an accident rather than a plan — what normally happens long before that is that the fighting costs your opponent the cards they needed.

**11.5** Losses are checked at the moment they occur, not continuously. A hand that is momentarily empty mid-Cleanup but refilled by a rally in the same phase is not a loss.

### 12.1 The round at a glance

```
@@FIG phases@@
1  PLAY CARDS        both choose face down → reveal → low initiative wins
                     (tie: current holder keeps it)

2  MOVE COMMANDERS   initiative first · up to 4 spaces · no enemy spaces

3  ISSUE COMMANDS    initiative player: issue all, then resolve all
                     then other player: issue all, then resolve all

                     · rear = defender routs now
                     · flank = defender wheels
                     · front = defender may fall back if faster

4  RESOLVE MELEE     initiative player picks the order
                     every engagement fights · both sides strike at once

5  CLEANUP           in-play cards → played pile
                     initiative player: rally? → burn 1 → take back
                                        → support check → routs
                     then other player: same
```

### 12.2 Quick tables

**Initiative** — lower wins; tie goes to the current holder.

**Movement costs**

|                                  | Cost                     |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| One step, orthogonal or diagonal | 1 Speed                  |
| One facing change, any angle     | 1 Flexibility            |
| Passing through a friendly unit  | combined Flexibility ≥ 4 |

**Engagement type** — engaging unit's facing vs. defender's facing

| Same or 45° off                   | 90° off                                 | Opposite                                     |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| **Rear** — defender routs at once | **Flank** — defender wheels, no retreat | **Front** — defender may fall back if faster |

**Melee support**

| Fighting unit is in supporter's… |        |
| -------------------------------- | ------ |
| front spaces                     | **+2** |
| flanking spaces                  | **+1** |
| back spaces                      | —      |

Supporter must be adjacent, unengaged, not behind the fighter, and not across a blocked diagonal.

**Ranged support** — +1 per supporter, flat. A supporter must have been ordered by the same command, must be able to make the shot itself, and spends its own shot to support.

**Combat results** — left to right, in printed order

| Retreat                | Reverse   | Rout                |
| ---------------------- | --------- | ------------------- |
| smallest backward move | turn 180° | removed; pay Morale |

If it routs, skip the other two. Reverse cannot happen while still engaged. A forced retreat with nowhere to go becomes a rout.

**Commitments** — +1 to the card's printed stat, always optional. Each moment accepts only some stats:

| Movement           | Front-engagement defence | Ranged                     | Melee                        |
| ------------------ | ------------------------ | -------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| Speed, Flexibility | Speed, Flexibility       | Range, Attack, Flexibility | Attack, Defence, Flexibility |

**You lose when** your hand is empty, when a Morale payment would take your last card, or when you have no units left on the board.

## 13. Index of awkward situations

Things that come up, in the order players tend to hit them.

**"Can I turn and then charge in the same order?"** Yes. Turn (1 Flexibility), step, turn again after the step if you have Flexibility left. You may turn once before your first step and once after each step. §5.2.2

**"Can I turn twice on the spot?"** No. One facing change per space occupied. §5.2.2

**"My unit has Flexibility 0."** It cannot turn, ever, under any circumstances short of a card giving it +1. It also cannot be ordered to make a front engagement unless it already happens to be squared up to the target. §5.2.3, §6.2.3

**"Can I walk around their flank and hit them in the back?"** Not in one order. A rear engagement requires that you were _already_ behind them when the move began. §6.2.2

**"They have a unit in my way and I'm faster."** You can pass through friendlies if the combined Flexibility is 4 or more. You can never pass through an enemy, and you can never pass through a space where two units are engaged. §5.4

**"Both units routed each other in melee."** Yes, that happens. Melee is simultaneous. Both are removed, both owners pay Morale. §7.2.1

**"My unit has to retreat but it's against the board edge."** It routs. §7.5, §9.1.6

**"There's an enemy behind my unit — can it still retreat?"** Only if that enemy is engaged, or is not facing your unit. An unengaged enemy facing your unit from behind cuts off the retreat entirely. §9.1.5

**"Can I retreat further than necessary to get somewhere useful?"** No. A retreat is always the smallest legal backward move: fewest turns, then fewest steps. §9.1.3

**"Can my archers shoot through my own line?"** Yes, for now. Line of sight is not yet in the game, so anything in the forward arc and within Range can be shot. §4.3.11

**"Can my archers shoot while engaged?"** No. §4.3.11

**"Do my archers shoot automatically?"** No. Shooting only happens when a Ranged Attack command orders it. A round in which you play a Movement card is a round in which nobody shoots. §4.3.11

**"Can I command the same unit twice?"** No — once per round, whether alone or as part of a line. §4.3.6

**"Can a line include a unit I already commanded?"** No, and the line stops there rather than jumping over it. §4.3.6

**"How far does a line reach from my general?"** The end you name first must be within inspiration range. The rest of the line need only match the trait and type restrictions — it can run right off into the distance. §4.3.5

**"My card says 4 units. Do I have to order four?"** No. It is a maximum. You may order fewer, or none. §4.3.2

**"Do the command's modifiers last into the melee phase?"** Yes, for the whole round, for the units that were actually commanded. §4.3.8

**"Does the round effect apply to units I didn't command?"** Yes — that is exactly what distinguishes it from the command's modifiers. It applies to any of your units that satisfy _its_ restrictions. §4.3.7

**"Can I commit the card I played this round?"** No. Only cards in hand. §8.6

**"Do committed cards come back?"** Yes, on your next rally, along with everything else in your discard pile. Only the one card burnt during a rally is gone forever. §10.3

**"Can I rally with an empty hand?"** Yes, provided you have something in your played pile to burn — and this is exactly the situation rallying exists for. But note §11.1: an empty hand is only a loss when it is actually checked, so the rally must happen before you are asked for anything. §10.3.3

**"If I never rally, do I ever lose units to the support check?"** No. The support check happens only after a rally. Declining to rally is a way to keep units on the board with a hand too thin to justify them — for exactly as long as you can afford to hold cards. §10.3.4

**"Does my opponent's rally affect my units?"** No. Each player checks only their own. §10.4

**"Two of my units both qualify for the last support place."** Your choice, and you are not required to make the choice that saves the most units. §10.4.5

**"I have exactly as many cards as the Morale I owe."** You lose. You must survive the payment, not merely make it. §11.2

## 14. Glossary

**Adjacent** — orthogonally or diagonally touching; up to eight spaces. §2.4.1

**Arc** — the widening cone in front of a unit, used for shooting. §2.4.3

**Attack value** — the total a unit brings to one combat: Attack plus modifiers plus support. §7.1

**Back spaces** — the three spaces behind a unit. §2.3.2

**Burn** — to remove a card from the game permanently. Happens once per rally. §10.3

**Commander** — the general piece. No facing, no combat value; the origin of inspiration range. §4.2

**Commit** — to discard a card from hand for +1 to a stat, for one action. Always optional. §8

**Discard pile** — cards spent on commitments and Morale. Returns entirely on a rally. §10.1

**Engagement** — two enemy units locked in the same space. Created in Phase 3, fought in Phase 4. §6

**Flanking spaces** — the two spaces directly left and right of a unit. §2.3.2

**Flexibility** — how many times a unit may change facing in one move. Also governs passing through friends. §5.1

**Front spaces** — the three spaces a unit faces. §2.3.2

**In play** — the card you revealed this round. Not in hand and cannot be committed, but its support is unaffected. §4.1.6

**Initiative** — acting first. Won by the lower card value; kept on a tie. §4.1

**Inspiration range** — how far from your commander a command or round effect reaches. §4.3.4

**Line** — a contiguous rank of 1–8 friendly units standing shoulder to shoulder, all facing the same direction or its opposite. §4.3.5

**Morale** — how many cards a unit's rout costs its owner. Also the army-building floor. §3.1.6, §9.3

**Played pile** — cards played in previous rounds, awaiting a rally. §10.1

**Rally** — burn one card from the played pile, take everything else back, then check support. §10.3

**Reverse** — turned 180° in place. §9.2

**Rout** — removed from the battle; pay Morale. §9.3

**Round Effect** — a standing modifier from your in-play card, applying to qualifying units whether commanded or not. §4.3.7

**Support (combat)** — the bonus adjacent friends lend to a fight. §7.3

**Support (unit)** — the number of units your cards can keep on the board, counted after a rally. Every card you own contributes unless it has been burnt. §10.4

**Threshold** — Retreat, Reverse, or Rout, in that order. Met when the attack value equals or exceeds it. §7.5

**Traits** — _formation, sword, spear, phalanx, skirmish, javelin, mounted, horse._ How cards address groups of units.

## 16. Open questions for playtest

Honest list of places where this draft is describing something provisional, incomplete, or possibly wrong. Each one is a decision waiting to be made, not a rule to be memorised.

**16.1 Facings that classify as nothing.** Engagement type is read from facings (§6.3.1), but the eight facings do not all map cleanly: _opposite_ is Front, the two at 90° are Flank, and _same_ plus the two at 45° are Rear. That leaves the two facings at **135°** classified as nothing at all. This is reachable: a unit stepping diagonally in from one of the defender's front spaces can arrive at 135° if it doesn't turn. §6.2.3 patches it by requiring the engaging unit to _end facing directly opposite_, but the underlying table still has a hole. Either widen Front to include 135°, or make the squaring-up turn genuinely mandatory rather than merely permitted.

**16.2 Reverse thresholds of 0.** Velites, Numidian Skirmishers and Numidian Cavalry all show a Reverse of 0, which under §7.5.1 means _any_ attack reverses them, including an attack of zero. Two things say this is a dash meaning "not applicable" that has become a literal zero. First, a threshold of 0 is always met, which makes it not a threshold. Second, it is the only place in the roster where the printed thresholds do not climb left to right — every other unit has Retreat ≤ Reverse ≤ Rout, and these three read 1, 0, 3. If the intent is "never reverses", the rule needs a "0 means never" clause or a distinct sentinel.

**16.3 Inspiration range of 0 vs. none.** A range of **—** means no restriction. A range of **0** means _within zero spaces of the commander_ — that is, sharing the commander's space. Several cards appear to use 0 where "no restriction" was intended. Worth a printing convention: leave the field blank for unrestricted, and never print 0 unless you really mean stacked.

**16.4 Defence commitments in melee.** §8.4 lets a melee commitment be made for Defence, and §8.5 says Defence raises thresholds. Confirm this is intended to be symmetrical with the ranged case, where the defender's commitment demonstrably protects them. A melee commitment currently reads most naturally as boosting your own blow; a Defence commitment boosting your own thresholds is a different thing and should be stated explicitly on the card.

**16.5 Whose commitment feeds the retreat comparison.** In a front engagement (§6.3.2) both the mover and the defender may commit for Speed, and the two Speeds are then compared. It must be unambiguous that each player's commitment applies to _their own_ unit only. As written here it does; verify the implementation agrees.

**16.6 Commanders cannot be harmed.** Nothing in this draft threatens a commander. Given how much of the card design hangs on inspiration range, a commander who can never be pressured makes every inspiration restriction a one-time positioning puzzle rather than an ongoing tension. Consider capture, displacement, or a commander who must accompany a unit.

**16.7 Terrain and line of sight.** Both are planned; neither is in the game yet. Board spaces already carry terrain, elevation and water, and no rule reads them; shooting ignores everything between firer and target. Every rule in this draft assumes bare, open, transparent ground. Both additions will change the value of Range, of the forward arc, and of fast troops considerably, so treat the shooting rules in particular as provisional.

**16.8 Deployment is a placeholder.** §3.2 is a symmetrical belt with no scenario structure, no alternating placement, and no hidden setup. It will not survive contact with real play.

**16.9 Turning cost.** §5.2.1 charges 1 Flexibility for a facing change of any angle, which makes a 180° wheel as cheap as a 45° pivot but limits how many times you may do it. The alternative — 1 per 45° — is a materially different game, particularly for Flexibility-1 phalanxes. Both are defensible; this draft documents the first. Decide deliberately.

**16.10 No draw-avoidance.** Simultaneous empty hands are a draw (§11.1). Confirm that is wanted, or give the initiative player the win.

**16.11 Engaged units and the command list.** §4.3.4 states that an engaged unit cannot be given an order of its own. The rules engine enforces this for ranged attacks but not for movement commands: the eligible-unit query returns friendly units from engaged spaces as well as free ones, so an engaged unit can currently be handed a movement order. The rule is settled; the check is missing.

**16.13 Engaged units inside a line.** Separate from §16.11 and genuinely undecided. A line is a rank standing shoulder to shoulder, and one unit of that rank being engaged does not stop it being part of the rank — arguably that is the point of a line. Three readings are available: the line **stops** at the engaged unit, as it does at an already-commanded one; the line **spans** the engaged unit, which stays put while the rest move; or the line spans it and the engaged unit is somehow carried along. The first is the simplest to adjudicate, the second is the most faithful to what a line represents, and the third needs a rule for what happens to the engagement. Until this is settled, §4.3.5 carries a note telling players to agree in advance.

**16.12 The random burn.** Rallying destroys one played card chosen at random (§10.3.2). This is the only randomness in the game and the only thing that needs a physical mixing step at the table. Worth confirming it should stay random rather than becoming a choice, an oldest-first rule, or a cost paid some other way — a chosen burn would make rally timing a cleaner decision but would remove the only reason a player ever loses a card they wanted to keep.
